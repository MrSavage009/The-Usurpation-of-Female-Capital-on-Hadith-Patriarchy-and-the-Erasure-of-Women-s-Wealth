# Qawāma and the Erasure of Female Ownership: A Revisionist Hypothesis on "Her Wealth" vs. "His Wealth"

---

## Abstract

This paper advances a revisionist hypothesis concerning the transmission and grading of a hadith on women's spending. It argues that the original prophetic utterance was "A woman should not spend **her wealth** without her husband's permission," and that this was later altered to "**his wealth**." The original preserves two things simultaneously: the wife's **ownership** of her wealth and the husband's **qawāma** over her spending — authority-responsibility under God, bounded by *maʿrūf*. The husband must grant permission unless there is a proper reason not to; the default is permission. The alteration to "his wealth" does two things: it **erases her ownership** by assuming household wealth is his, and it **trivializes the ruling** — of course she cannot spend his wealth without permission; the duty of consultation disappears. The erasure of female capital is therefore specific to the "his wealth" version. Male permission over her spending is qawāma, not usurpation; male assumption of her ownership is erasure.

The paper further argues that the classical tradition made a **two-step error** regarding qawāma. The verse 4:34 uses the **verb** *anfaqū* — "they spend" — from the root *n-f-q*, whose concrete meaning is the burrow with two openings, the hidden exit, the unexpected outflow. The tradition first reified the verb into the technical noun *nafaqa* and narrowed it to money; second, it defined qawāma by the reified noun, producing what the paper calls the **dollar reduction** — authority purchased by allowance.

The paper identifies **four candidates** for what *infaq* invokes: the covering of the unforeseen, the absorption of impact, the expenditure of self through the hidden passage, and the **guarding against the hidden exit** — the wife's capacity to leave the union through a channel the husband does not control. The first three read the hidden door as the channel through which something emerges toward the husband. The fourth reads it as the channel through which the wife may exit away from the union. Together they give the full structure of the *ʿilla*: qawāma is authority because of the burden of the duty *and* because of the threat to guard against.

The paper also recovers the **structural reading of the mosque hadith**: a boy assumed standing to regulate women's movement, and the Prophet ﷺ rebuked only the specific application (the mosque), not the standing. The general qawāma over movement is therefore presumed. This is a **plain reading of the text**, not a hypothesis.

The paper distinguishes throughout between **observation** (texts, chains, biographies, socio-historical records), **interpretation** (the grading system), and **plain readings** (what the text says). The "her wealth" claim is a hypothesis — the best explanation of the observations. The mosque hadith reading is an observation. The paper invites revision if a better model emerges.

## Preface: Method, Grading, and the Status of Claims

### The Scientific Method: Observation and Interpretation

The scientific method rests on a disciplined distinction between **observation** and **interpretation**. Observation is what is measured, recorded, repeated. Interpretation is the explanatory model imposed on those observations. A thermometer reading is observation; the theory of heat is interpretation. A census count is observation; the demographic transition model is interpretation.

The humility of the method lies in its willingness to say: *This is what we observed. This is how we measured it. This is the error margin. This is the model we currently use. If a better model comes, we will abandon this one.* The observation remains; the interpretation changes. Newtonian mechanics was not wrong in its observations of falling apples; it was wrong in its interpretation of Mercury's orbit. Einstein did not discard the apple; he recontextualized it.

This is not relativism. It is **fallibilism**. It recognizes that all measurement is biased by instrument, observer, sample, and environment. The good scientist asks: *What is the error bar? What is the selection effect? What is the confirmation bias?* The good scientist does not say: *The theory is true because it is cutting edge.* The good scientist says: *The theory is useful until a better one arrives.*

**Scientism** is the inversion of this method. It takes interpretations and theories and treats them as cutting-edge truths. It does not say: *If better evidence arrives, we will change our theory gladly.* It says: *The theory is truth. Observations that contradict it are anomalies to be explained away, ignored, or suppressed.* Scientism is not science. It is a herald of truth that dogmatically portrays models as reality and then only considers observations that match the outcome of the theory.

### The Three Layers of Observation in Hadith Science

The hadith sciences have three layers of observation:

1. **Records of quotes** – the *matn* (the text of the report: what the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said or done).
2. **Records of chains of narration** – the *isnād* (the chain of names: who heard it from whom, back to the Companion, back to the Prophet ﷺ).
3. **Biographies of all people in the chain** – the *rijāl* (the narrators: their trustworthiness, memory, accuracy, reliability, and the circumstances of their transmission).

These three layers are observations. They are records. They are data. The chain of Ibn Majah 2295 is an observation. The chain of Ibn Majah 2296 is an observation. The biography of Ismail bin Ayyash is an observation. The biography of Abu Salamah bin 'Abd al-Rahman is an observation. The biography of Yahya bin Abi Kathir is an observation. The text "A woman should not spend her wealth without her husband's permission" is an observation. Another record allows us to observe the text "A woman should not spend his wealth without his permission." Both observations are possible. But it is likely, given the proximity of the text and the similarities in the chains of narration, that one of them is wrong — a transfer error from one original utterance of the Prophet ﷺ. The scholar who collects these records is doing descriptive work. He is not yet grading. He is observing.

### Grading as Interpretation

**What grading is.** Grading is the act of assigning a rank to a hadith. The ranks are familiar: *sahīh* (sound), *hasan* (good), *daʿīf* (weak), *mawdūʿ* (fabricated). The rank is a judgment about the hadith's reliability. It is not a property of the hadith itself. It is a verdict pronounced upon it.

The grader takes several inputs. He examines the continuity of the chain (*ittisāl*): does each narrator actually meet the next? He examines the trustworthiness of the narrators (*ʿadāla*): are they morally upright? He examines their precision (*dabt*): do they transmit accurately? He looks for hidden defects (*ʿilal*): are there subtle flaws that undermine the chain? He looks for anomalous content (*shudhūdh*): does the text contradict stronger reports? He looks for corroboration (*mutābaʿāt* and *shawāhid*): are there parallel chains that support it?

Each of these inputs requires judgment. None is a measurement. The grader does not put the chain on a scale and read a number. He reads biographies, weighs reports about narrators, assesses probabilities, and pronounces a verdict. The verdict is the grading. The grading is the interpretation.

**Why grading is interpretation, not observation.** The distinction is disciplined. An observation is what can be measured, recorded, repeated. A thermometer reading is an observation. A census count is an observation. The chain of Ibn Majah 2295 is an observation. The biography of Ismail bin Ayyash is an observation. The text "A woman should not spend her wealth without her husband's permission" is an observation.

A grading is not an observation. To say a hadith is *sahīh* is not to report a measurement. It is to impose a model on the observations. The model is useful. It helps organize the data. But it is not the data. The grading is a human judgment, embedded in a human society, shaped by human assumptions.

This is not a controversial claim. The hadith scholars themselves distinguished between *riwāya* (transmission) and *dirāya* (assessment). Transmission is the collection of observations. Assessment is the interpretation of those observations. The two are not the same. The scholar who collects chains is doing descriptive work. The scholar who grades them is doing interpretive work.

The confusion enters when the grading is treated as if it were the observation. When the scholar says "this hadith is *daʿīf*," he is not reporting a fact about the hadith. He is reporting his judgment. The judgment may be correct. It may be incorrect. It may be contested. But it is not an observation. It is an interpretation.

**How grading works as a system.** The grading system rests on assumptions. Some are methodological. Some are theological. Some are cultural. The assumptions are not always stated. They are often invisible to the grader himself.

*Assumption 1: Oral transmission is superior to written transmission.* The scholar al-Awza'i said: "This science was once a noble matter, when people still received it in lectures. But when it entered into books, it lost its shine." The assumption is that direct hearing (*samā'*) is stronger than written transmission (*kitābah*). A report transmitted through written materials is weaker than one transmitted through oral audition.

*Assumption 2: A continuous chain is stronger than a broken chain.* If every narrator in the chain actually met the next, the report is stronger. If there is a gap (*inqitā'*), the report is weaker. This assumption is reasonable. But it is an assumption. It privileges continuity over content.

*Assumption 3: A trustworthy narrator is more reliable than an untrustworthy one.* This seems obvious. But the assessment of trustworthiness is itself an interpretation. The same narrator can be graded *thiqah* by one critic and *daʿīf* by another. The judgment depends on the critic's standards, his sources, his context.

*Assumption 4: A report that contradicts stronger reports is anomalous (*shādhdh*).* This assumption privileges the majority. If most chains say one thing and one chain says another, the majority is presumed correct. This assumption is reasonable in many cases. But in a society that prefers the tame to the demanding, the majority may be the softening. The majority version may be the one that serves power.

*Assumption 5: The grading of earlier scholars is binding on later scholars.* This is the assumption of *taqlīd*. If Ibn Hajar said a hadith is *daʿīf*, later scholars repeat the verdict without re-examining the observations. The assumption is that the earlier scholar was better positioned, better trained, better informed. This may be true. But it is an assumption. It turns a judgment into a dogma.

These assumptions are not neutral. They shape the grading. They determine which reports are promoted and which are marginalized. They are the invisible architecture of the grading system.

**The same observations, different gradings.** The decisive demonstration that grading is interpretation is that the same observations yield different gradings depending on the assumptions brought to them.

- *Example 1: The same narrator, different verdicts.* Imam Ahmad said of a certain narrator: "He is *thiqah*." Imam Yahya ibn Ma'in said of the same narrator: "He is *daʿīf*." Both critics examined the same biography. Both heard the same reports. They reached different verdicts. The difference is not in the observations. It is in the interpretation.
- *Example 2: The same chain, different assessments.* The chain of a certain hadith is graded *muttasil* (continuous) by the school of Madinah and *munqati'* (broken) by the school of Kufa. The difference is not in the names. It is in the criteria for continuity. Did the narrator meet his teacher? The Madinan school says yes. The Kufan school says no. The observations are the same. The gradings differ.
- *Example 3: The same tadlīs, different treatment.* A narrator practices *tadlīs* — he uses ambiguous wording to conceal a gap in the chain. One critic treats this as fatal: the hadith is *daʿīf*. Another critic treats it as acceptable: the narrator is known to be reliable, and his *tadlīs* does not undermine the report. The same observation. Different gradings.
- *Example 4: The same text, different verdicts.* A hadith is graded *sahīh* by Bukhari and *daʿīf* by Abu Hatim al-Razi. The same text. The same chain. Different verdicts. The difference is in the assumptions. Bukhari accepts the narrator. Abu Hatim rejects him. The observations are identical. The interpretations diverge.

These disagreements are not anomalies. They are the norm. The grading system is not a machine that produces uniform outputs from uniform inputs. It is a human practice that produces different outputs from the same inputs depending on the assumptions of the grader. This is what it means for grading to be interpretation.

**How grading becomes dogma.** The progression from judgment to dogma is gradual. It has identifiable stages.

1. **A scholar makes a judgment.** Ibn Hajar examines the chain of a hadith. He finds a defect. He grades it *daʿīf*. The judgment is his interpretation of the observations.
2. **The judgment is recorded.** Ibn Hajar writes his verdict in his book. The verdict is now a text. It can be cited. It can be repeated.
3. **Later scholars repeat the judgment.** A later scholar reads Ibn Hajar. He does not re-examine the chain. He does not check the biography. He repeats: "Ibn Hajar said it is *daʿīf*." The judgment is now a citation.
4. **The judgment becomes consensus.** More scholars repeat the verdict. The verdict becomes the standard view. It is now *ijmā'* — consensus.
5. **The consensus becomes infallible.** The consensus is treated as binding. To question it is to deviate. The verdict is now dogma.
6. **The original observations are forgotten.** The chain, the biography, the context — all are forgotten. The only thing that remains is the verdict. The grading is now the reality.
7. **The grading is treated as if it were the observation.** The scholar says: "This hadith is *daʿīf*." He does not say: "Ibn Hajar graded it *daʿīf*." He does not say: "The chain has a defect that Ibn Hajar judged fatal." He says: "It is *daʿīf*." The interpretation has become the observation. The map has become the territory.

This is the process the paper calls *taqlīd* repetition. It is not unique to hadith science. It happens in every discipline. But it is especially dangerous in hadith science because the stakes are religious. A *daʿīf* hadith is dismissed. A *sahīh* hadith is binding. The grading determines what is taught, what is practiced, what is believed. The grading is not a neutral tool. It is a mechanism of authority.

**The samā'/kitābah collapse.** A concrete example of grading-system failure is the collapse of the *samā'*/*kitābah* distinction.

The scholars themselves distinguished between "heard/audited" transmission (*al-riwāyah al-masmū'ah*, *samā'*) and "merely written" transmission (*kitābah*). The written word was seen as deficient because it "needs support," because "the author has to intervene if the written text was to be correctly understood," and because traditions recorded in writing could "fall into the wrong hands." Al-Awza'i said: "This science was once a noble matter, when people still received it in lectures. But when it entered into books, it lost its shine."

A report transmitted through written materials is **not as strong** as a report transmitted through direct oral *samā'*. But it is **not a lie**. It is not a fabrication. It is a category between the strong true positive of *samā'* and the true negative of fabrication.

The grading system collapses this distinction. It treats any defect as evidence of weakness or falsehood. It does not recognize that written transmission produces a distinct category of evidence: weaker than oral, but not false. It reduces the spectrum to a binary: strong or weak, authentic or fabricated.

The "her wealth" chains are a case in point. Abu Salamah's *irsāl* and Yahya bin Abi Kathir's *tadlīs* are real defects. But they are defects of *kitābah* and irregular transmission, not of fabrication. The grading system labels the chains *daʿīf*. The label is not wrong in the sense that the chains are weaker than the canonical chains. But the label collapses a nuanced reality into a blunt verdict. It says "weak" when it should say "weaker than oral, but not false." It dismisses when it should distinguish.

**The case of the "her wealth" version.** The grading of the "her wealth" version as *daʿīf* is the specific case that demonstrates the general problem.

The chain defects are real. Ismail bin Ayyash has a conditional vulnerability. Abu Umamah's transmission school is weak. Abu Salamah practices *irsāl*. Yahya bin Abi Kathir practices *tadlīs*. These are observations.

The grading of *daʿīf* is an interpretation of these observations. It says: these defects are sufficient to render the hadith unreliable. The interpretation rests on assumptions: that oral transmission is superior to written, that continuity is stronger than brokenness, that the majority version is the original, that the canonical version is authentic.

The paper argues that a different set of assumptions yields a different grading. If the "her wealth" version is the demanding original, and the "his wealth" version is the tame softening, then the defects in the "her" chains are not signs of falsehood. They are signs of marginalization. The chains are broken because the text was inconvenient. The narrators concealed because the text was dangerous. The grading of *daʿīf* is not a neutral verdict. It is a judgment shaped by the assumption that the tame version is original.

The paper does not claim that the "her wealth" version is *sahīh* by the standards of the grading system. It claims that the grading system's standards are themselves interpretations. The same observations — the same chain defects, the same narrator biographies — could yield a different grading under a different model. The paper proposes that model.

**The sheikh al-hadith as taqlid repeater.** The practical consequence of confusing grading with observation is the figure the paper calls the *taqlid* repeater.

The *sheikh alim al-hadith* should consider and analyze the records. He should ask: *Why was this chain broken? Why was this narrator concealing? Was it because the text was inconvenient? Was it because the stricter ruling was marginalized?* He should analyze the bias in the measurement. He should ask: *Who graded this? What were their incentives? What was the political context?* He should do what the scientist does: separate observation from interpretation, and then analyze the interpretation for bias.

But instead, the *sheikh alim* is often a **taqlid repeater**. He does not analyze. He repeats. He says: *Ibn Hajar said it is daʿīf, so it is daʿīf.* He does not ask: *Why did Ibn Hajar say that? What were his assumptions? What was his context?* He treats the grading as if it were the observation. He confuses the map with the territory. He confuses the interpretation with the data.

This is the essence of scientism in the hadith sciences. The grading is treated as cutting-edge truth. Observations that contradict it are anomalies to be explained away, ignored, or suppressed. The original hadith on women's spending is, under this hypothesis, one such observation. It preserves a stricter ruling. So, the hypothesis argues, it is marginalized. The scholars who graded it weak, under this reading, were not defending authenticity. They were defending the softened version.

The "fake humility" of the scholars is the key. They said: *We only transmit what is authentic.* But they defined authenticity in a way that excluded the demanding texts. They defined it in a way that served the practical order. The canon, under this reading, is not the pure word of God. It is the word of the Abbasid scholarly class, shaped by its political economy. The revisionist must read the canon with care. The revisionist must look for the texts that were marginalized.

**The duty of the scholar.** The duty of the scholar is to **build humbly on ever-changing flawed interpretation by respecting only the observations**.

The observations are the chains. The observations are the biographies. The observations are the texts. The interpretations are the gradings. The scholar must respect the observations. He must analyze the biases in the measurement. He must say: *This is what we have. This is how we got it. This is the error margin. This is the model we currently use. If a better model comes, we will change it gladly.*

The *taqlid* repeater does not do this. He says: *The model is truth. The grading is settled. The consensus is infallible.* He is the scientismist of the hadith. He is the herald of truth who dogmatically portrays theories as truth. He is the one who only considers observations that match the outcome of the theory.

This is also the error of modern daʿwa. The scholar leads with authority (the grade) without first establishing *maʿrūf* (what the observations actually say). Authority without established good is tyranny. The grade without the evidence is dogma. The sheikh al-hadith must first establish what the text says, what the chain shows, what the biography records. Then, and only then, may he offer a grade. But the grade is not the observation. The grade is the interpretation. And the interpretation must be accountable to the observation. This is the daʿwa method the Quran teaches: wisdom first, then authority. *Maʿrūf* first, then *qawāma*. The good established, then the structure that protects it.

**Grading as tool vs. grading as dogma.** The paper does not reject grading wholesale. Grading is a useful tool. It helps organize the data. It helps distinguish stronger reports from weaker ones. It helps the scholar navigate a vast and complex tradition.

But grading is a tool, not a verdict. It is an interpretation, not an observation. It is accountable to the observations, not the other way around. When grading is used as a tool, it serves the scholar. When grading becomes dogma, it masters him.

The distinction is between two orientations. **Grading as tool:** the scholar uses the grading to organize the data. He asks: *What does the chain show? What does the biography record? What does the text say?* He treats the grading as a hypothesis, not a fact. He is willing to revise it if the observations demand it. **Grading as dogma:** the scholar treats the grading as the data. He asks: *What did Ibn Hajar say? What is the consensus?* He repeats the verdict without re-examining the observations. He treats the grading as infallible. He is not willing to revise it, even if the observations demand it.

The paper's position is clear: grading is a tool. It is useful. It is not infallible. It is not an observation. It is an interpretation. The scholar must use it, not worship it. He must respect the observations, not the verdicts. He must build humbly on ever-changing flawed interpretation. He must say: *This is what we have. If a better model comes, we will change it gladly.* This is the scientific method. This is the opposite of the dogma of the grading system. This is the opposite of scientism.

### Hypothesis Status of This Thesis

**The hypothesis, stated once.** This thesis advances a single hypothesis: **the original prophetic utterance was "A woman should not spend her wealth without her husband's permission," and this was later altered to "his wealth."** The original preserves the wife's ownership and places her spending under the husband's qawāma. The alteration erases her ownership and trivializes the ruling. Everything else in this paper — the chain analysis, the socio-historical reading, the critique of grading, the daʿwa appendix — is either evidence for this hypothesis or a consequence of it. The hypothesis itself is one sentence. The paper is the argument that it is the best explanation of the observations.

**What the thesis is not claiming.** The thesis does **not** claim to have observed the original utterance. It does not claim to have excavated a manuscript proving the "her wealth" version is authentic. It does not claim that the canonical "his wealth" version is a fabrication, a forgery, or a conscious lie. It does not claim that the scholars who graded the "her wealth" chains weak were personally corrupt, paid off, or acting in bad faith. It does not claim that the Abbasid state issued a decree suppressing a text. It does not claim that the alteration was a single act at a single moment. What it claims is narrower and more defensible: **the "her wealth" version is the best explanation of the observations, and the "his wealth" version is best explained as a later softening.** That is a claim about explanatory power, not about manuscript evidence. It is a claim about which model accounts for the most data with the fewest anomalies. It is a hypothesis, not a discovery.

**The observations and the interpretation.** The distinction is disciplined and must not be blurred.

*The observations* are the texts, the chains, the biographies, and the socio-historical records. They are what can be cited, quoted, dated, and checked: the matn of Ibn Majah 2295 and 2296; the matn of Bukhari 5195 and Muslim 1026; the chains of each; the biographies of Ismail bin Ayyash, Abu Salamah, Yahya bin Abi Kathir, Abu Hurayrah; the socio-historical records of Umayyad and Abbasid court cases, *'urf* practices, the household as a corporate unit under male headship.

*The interpretation* is the argument imposed on these observations: that the "her wealth" version is original, that the "his wealth" version is a softening, that the chain defects are ordinary transmission artifacts rather than signs of falsehood, that the marginalization of the "her" chains reflects the preferences of a scholarly class rather than the verdict of neutral science.

The observations are not in dispute. Both versions exist. Both chains exist. Both biographies exist. The dispute is entirely at the level of interpretation.

**A crucial distinction: hypotheses vs. plain readings.** Not every claim in this paper is a hypothesis. The structural reading of the mosque hadith — Ibn 'Umar's report that a son of his said "We will prevent them!" and was rebuked — is a **plain reading of the text**. The text shows a boy assuming standing to regulate women's movement. It shows Ibn 'Umar rebuking the specific application, not the standing. It shows the general qawāma over movement presumed by the structure of the exchange. These are not hypotheses. They are observations. The hypothesis status of the paper applies to the "her wealth" claim. It does not apply to the structural reading of Hadith 16. The paper must be explicit about this distinction. Conflating observations with hypotheses undermines the method.

**The supports.** The hypothesis is supported by several independent lines of reasoning. They are listed here compactly; each is developed in the body of the paper.

1. **The n/n+k model correctly applied.** The canon treats the tame "his wealth" version as (n) and the demanding "her wealth" version as (k). The paper argues this classification is inverted: the demanding version is the true (n), and the tame version is the (k) promoted to (n).
2. **The logic of triviality vs. demandingness.** "She cannot spend his wealth without his permission" is legally obvious and requires no prophetic intervention. "She cannot spend her wealth without his permission" is distinctive and imposes a duty of consultation. The more demanding a ruling, the more likely it is to be softened.
3. **The existence of two independent marginal chains.** Ibn Majah 2295 and 2296 preserve the "her"-type wording through different Companions, different intermediaries, and different regions. Two broken fossils are stronger evidence than one.
4. **The documented transmission habits of the narrators.** Abu Salamah's *irsāl*, Yahya bin Abi Kathir's *tadlīs* under political pursuit, and Ismail bin Ayyash's conditional reliability are all recorded in the biographical sources. They explain the defects without requiring conspiracy.
5. **The kitābah/samā' distinction.** The grading system collapses written transmission into the same category as fabrication. The paper restores the nuance: a report can be weaker than oral *samā'* without being false.
6. **The socio-historical praxis.** Male control of women's spending was the norm. The original hadith gave religious cover to qawāma; the altered hadith gave cover to the erasure of ownership.
7. **The political economy of the Abbasid canon.** The state patronized the scholars; the scholars preferred the tame version; the canon reflects that preference.
8. **The feminazi/peacock double-agent logic.** The softened version is celebrated as protecting women's property while actually erasing their ownership. The double agent conceals the erasure.
9. **The coherent definition of qawāma.** Chapter 2's account — qawāma as authority-responsibility under God, bounded by *maʿrūf*, default permission unless proper reason — makes the original "her wealth" version intelligible as a qawāma ruling and the "his wealth" version intelligible as its softening.
10. **The two-step error.** The verse uses the verb *anfaqū*, from the root *n-f-q*, whose concrete meaning is the hidden door. The tradition reified the verb into the noun *nafaqa* and defined qawāma by the reified noun. The original "her wealth" version presupposes the correct understanding of qawāma as covering the hidden door, not paying an allowance.
11. **The four candidates for *infaq*.** The covering of the unforeseen, the absorption of impact, the expenditure of self, and the guarding against the hidden exit. Together they give the full structure of the *ʿilla*: qawāma is authority because of the burden of the duty and because of the threat to guard against.

These supports are cumulative, not independent in the strict sense. They reinforce one another. None alone proves the hypothesis. Together they make it the best explanation available.

**What would count as revision.** The hypothesis is falsifiable. It would be revised or abandoned under any of the following conditions:

1. **Manuscript evidence.** If a manuscript with a strong, continuous, orally-audited chain were discovered supporting "his wealth" as the original wording, the hypothesis would be seriously weakened.
2. **Chain provenance.** If the "her wealth" chains were shown to derive from a single known source, or to be later fabrications with identifiable authors, the two-fossil argument would collapse.
3. **Biographical reversal.** If the documented defects in the "her wealth" chains were shown to be signs of fabrication rather than transmission artifacts — for example, if Abu Salamah's *irsāl* were shown to be systematic concealment of known forgeries — the revisionist reading would fail.
4. **Socio-historical disconfirmation.** If the historical record showed that male control of women's spending was not the norm, or that women's ownership was never absorbed into male-controlled estates, the praxis argument would lose its force.
5. **A more parsimonious model.** If a rival interpretation accounted for the same observations with fewer assumptions — for example, if the "his wealth" version could be shown to be original while still explaining the existence of the "her" chains — the hypothesis would be replaced.

The hypothesis is not immune to evidence. It is accountable to the observations. This is what distinguishes it from the dogma it critiques.

**The weak-label clarification.** A separate point must be made about the word "weak." The paper does **not** call the "her wealth" version a "weak hadith." That is the language of the grading system, and the paper rejects it. Under the hypothesis, the "her wealth" version is the **original hadith** — the authentic prophetic utterance. The label "weak" is itself the dogma. It is the post hoc rationalization of a scholarly class loyal to a priori commitments. When the grading system labels the original hadith "weak," it is not reporting an observation. It is enforcing a verdict. The paper rejects that verdict.

The *sheikh alim al-hadith*, in this reading, is not an evaluator of records. He is a **dogma repeater**. He does not ask why the chain was broken. He does not ask what incentives shaped the grading. He does not ask whether the text was inconvenient. He repeats: *Ibn Hajar said it is daʿīf, so it is daʿīf.* He treats the interpretation as if it were the observation. He confuses the map with the territory. This is scientism in the hadith sciences — the herald of truth who dogmatically portrays models as reality and then only considers observations that match the theory.

The paper's position is therefore double: the "her wealth" version is the original, under the hypothesis; and the "weak" label is the dogma, under the reading. Both claims are interpretations, not observations. Both are advanced as the best explanation, not as facts.

**The humility formula.** This entire thesis is a hypothesis — with the exception of the structural reading of the mosque hadith, which is a plain reading of the text. It is supported by the arguments above. It is the best explanation of the observations that the author can offer. But it is not a fact. It is not an observation. It is an interpretation. If a better model comes — one that accounts for the same observations with greater coherence, greater parsimony, or fewer anomalies — this one will be abandoned gladly. This is the scientific method. This is the opposite of the dogma of the grading system. This is the opposite of scientism. The reader is invited to test the hypothesis, challenge it, and propose better models. The observations remain. The interpretation is accountable to them. That is the whole of the method.

### A Crucial Clarification About Qawāma, Ownership, and Spending Permission

This paper distinguishes between authentic qawāma — the husband's authority-responsibility under God, bounded by *maʿrūf* (that which is certainly good) — and its distortions. Authentic qawāma is a burden of service, not a claim of dominance. It is the authority to protect in that which is certainly good, and to leave her free in everything else.

The paper also distinguishes three layers that the "his wealth" version collapses:

| Layer | "Her wealth" version (original, under the hypothesis) | "His wealth" version (altered, under the hypothesis) |
|---|---|---|
| **Ownership** | Hers. Absolute. She inherits, earns, dowries, owns. | Erased. Household wealth is assumed to be his. |
| **Spending permission** | Husband's qawāma. She seeks permission. He grants unless there is a proper *maʿrūf* reason not to. Default is permission. | Trivialized. Of course she cannot spend his wealth. The qawāma duty of consultation disappears. |
| **Effect** | Preserves female capital and qawāma. | Erases female capital and softens qawāma. |

The husband does not own her wealth, does not spend it, and may not deny arbitrarily. His qawāma is over the timing and manner of her spending, not over the substance of her property. Ownership matters for divorce, remarriage, exit options, long-term spending vision, and bargaining power. The "his wealth" version removes this.

The paper does **not** treat male control of women's spending as a patriarchal usurpation. It treats it as qawāma. The wife should seek permission. The husband should grant it unless there is a proper reason not to. The default is permission. Arbitrary denial is not qawāma. It is a distortion.

### Qawāma Is General, Not Marital-Only

Q 4:34 says *al-rijāl qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ* — men are qawwāmūn over women. The wording is general: *rijāl / nisāʾ*, not *zawj / zawja*. The classical scholars who reduced it to husband and wife made an interpretive choice. The paper rejects that reduction.

Qawāma is the authority-responsibility of men over women in the domains where they carry responsibility. The forms are many: husband over wife; father over dependent daughter; grandfather over granddaughter; son over mother; brother over sister; uncle over niece; neighbor over widow; community or state over destitute women; the male community over women's public movement.

The **order of responsibility** is: husband → father → grandfather → son → brother → uncle. This is a practical and legal specification, not the literal wording of the verse. It is grounded in the general principle, not in a single text that lists the order.

Different relationships are governed by different categories:

- **Qawāma** — authority-responsibility, primary in marriage, secondary in communal protection.
- **Wilāya** — marriage guardianship. Concerns contracting a marriage. Varies by school. Requires the woman's consent in most cases. Does not give general authority over her person, wealth, or movements.
- **Nafaqa** — maintenance. An obligation to provide. Not authority over property.
- **Protection from harm** — a general moral duty. Not a male rank.

### Ordered Qawāma vs. Disordered Qawāma

**Ordered qawāma** is authority-responsibility under God, bounded by *maʿrūf*. It protects in that which is certainly good and leaves her free in everything else. It does not erase her ownership. It does not deny her property. It does not imprison her. It does not beat her. It grants permission unless there is a proper reason not to.

**Disordered qawāma** is the abuse of authority: control, isolation, denial of property, arbitrary denial of permission, forced marriage, honor killing, beating, imprisonment. It is authority without *maʿrūf*. It is protection that is actually harm.

The paper critiques disordered qawāma. It affirms ordered qawāma. It does not affirm a general male hierarchy. The erasure of female ownership was an act of disordered qawāma.

## Chapter 1: Introduction: The Iconoclast Turn

This thesis advances a radical revisionist reading of the Islamic textual tradition concerning women's wealth, marital authority, and the transmission of hadith. It argues that the authentic prophetic utterance originally restricted a woman from spending **her own wealth** without her husband's permission, and that this original text was later altered to read **"his wealth"** by a scholarly class — the "peacock softeners" — who preferred the tame version to the demanding one.

The method employed here is one of **hermeneutical suspicion**. It does not accept the canonical grading of hadith as neutral. It reads the marginal narrations, the broken chains, and the socio-historical context as evidence of marginalization. It applies the **n/n+k model** — where (n) is the certain true positive and (k) is the variant requiring analysis and scholarly work — but disputes the canonical classification: here, the canon treats the tame "his wealth" version as (n), and the demanding "her wealth" version as (k). The thesis argues that this classification is inverted: the demanding version is the true (n), and the tame version is the (k) that has been falsely promoted to (n) by the softening.

The methodological framework established in the preface is essential. The observations are the texts, the chains, the biographies. The interpretations are the gradings. The taqlid repeater confuses the two, treating the grading as if it were the observation. The revisionist scholar must return to the observations, analyze the biases in the measurement, and be willing to say: *We were wrong before. We may be wrong again. The original hadith may be the truth. The canonical hadith may be the softening.*

The paper begins with a hadith that is often read as a limit on male authority, but which is better read as a specification of qawāma. Ibn 'Umar reported the Prophet ﷺ said: "Do not prevent the female slaves of Allah from praying in the mosque." A son of his said, "We will prevent them!" Ibn 'Umar became very angry and rebuked him.

The hadith shows a **boy assuming standing** to regulate women's movement. He did not say, "That is not my concern." He said, "We will prevent them." He assumed standing. Ibn 'Umar rebuked him — but the rebuke was **specific to the mosque**, not to the standing. The general qawāma over women's movement is therefore **presumed** by the structure of the exchange. The mosque is a specified exemption, not a denial of the qawāma function.

This is a **plain reading of the text**, not a hypothesis. The boy's assumption is in his words. The specificity of the rebuke is in Ibn 'Umar's anger and its object. The exemption implies the rule. The distinction is fundamental and largely absent from scholarly treatment. Modern readings tend to treat the hadith as a limit on male authority, not as a specification within it. They emphasize the exemption and overlook the presumption. The paper recovers the structural reading.

The distinction matters for the paper's broader argument. The qawāma function — regulating women's movement out of the home — is **timeless and abstract**. It is not specified in the abstract. The mosque is a **timeless specified exemption**. The **applications** of the function are culturally and temporally dependent. What movements to limit, when, and how are matters to be determined within *maʿrūf*. The function itself is not in question. The applications are.

The paper also argues that the tradition made a **two-step error** regarding qawāma. ## Chapter 2: Qawāma: Authority-Responsibility Under God

### The Principle of Protection — Certain Good vs. Cultural Distortion

Qawāma is the authority-responsibility to protect in that which is **certainly good** (*maʿrūf*). It is not a license for harm disguised as protection. The *qawwām* stands over the woman entrusted to his care to prevent clear, certain, and permanent harm — and to leave her free in everything else.

This is why cultures resist qawāma. It implies authority to intervene. But cultures reject intervention when their own norms and definitions of "protection" are invasive, dubious, or proven harmful. They recoil not from qawāma itself but from a distorted image of it.

Examples of distorted "protection" that is not qawāma — distortions from cultures that reject divine guidance, not from Islamic sources:

- **Forced psychiatric drugging:** A parent or doctor forces SSRIs or ADHD medication on a child to control behavior, not to treat clear pathology. The intervention carries known risks, suppresses autonomy, and is framed as protection. This is not *maʿrūf*.
- **Forced marriage:** Framed as protecting a daughter's future. It is coercion, not protection.
- **Conversion therapy:** Framed as protecting the soul. It is psychological harm, not protection.
- **Forced sterilization:** Framed as protecting family or society. It is bodily violation, not protection.
- **Surgery or cauterization with clear risks:** Framed as protection, but the means inflict harm that Islam does not permit under the guise of protection.
- **Eugenic abortion:** Framed as protecting the family from burden. It is killing, not protection.

These are not qawāma. They are betrayals of it. A *qawwām* may not claim protection to violate bodily autonomy over medical decisions, to force invasive procedures, or to impose cultural practices that are themselves harmful.

Contrast this with stopping a person from cutting her own arm with a razor. That is not an invasive intervention with dubious benefit. It is stopping a clear, active, permanent harm — and leaving her free to do anything else. The *qawwām* removes the razor, blocks the harmful peers, and stays with her. He does not drug her into submission or cut her flesh to save her.

The key line: **Qawāma means authority to intervene to protect — but only in that which is certainly good.** Cultures will reject qawāma when their own definitions of protection are infringing, dubious, or simply proven harmful. Islam rejects those definitions first. Protection must be in *maʿrūf*, by means that are themselves good, and never by inflicting harm in the name of preventing it.

### Etymology of Rijāl and Nisāʾ

*Rijāl* is the plural of *rajul* (man), from the triliteral root **r-j-l**, which denotes the foot or leg, and by extension standing, walking, uprightness, and manhood. *Nisāʾ* is the plural of *imraʾah* (woman); its origin is debated, often linked to **n-s-w** or **n-s-y** (forgetfulness, delicacy) or to **ʾ-n-s** (humanity, sociability). When paired, *rijāl nisāʾ* signifies "men and women" collectively, a merism encompassing the human community.

### The Verse

> **الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ**
> *al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ*
> "Men are qawwāmūn over women."

The verse provides its own *ʿilla* (its own stated reason, the cause it gives for the ruling): **bimā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍ wa bimā anfaqū min amwālihim** — "because Allah preferred some over others, and because they spend from their wealth."

*Qawwām* is the intensive form of the root **q-w-m** (to stand). A *qāʾim* is one who stands; a *qawwām* is one who stands over, manages, guards, supervises, maintains, and is responsible for. The preposition **ʿalā** means "over."

**Qawwām = authority-responsibility.** Not a wallet. Not a throne. A trust (*amāna*) under God.

The wording is general: *al-rijāl* / *al-nisāʾ*, not *al-zawj* / *al-zawja* (husband/wife). The primary context is as written — men and women as categories. The marital reading is a specification, not the primary meaning. The classical scholars who reduced it to husband and wife made an interpretive choice. The paper rejects that reduction. Qawāma is the authority-responsibility of men over women in the domains where they carry responsibility. The marital form is one specification, not the primary meaning. The other forms — father over dependent daughter, son over mother, brother over sister, community over women — are equally qawāma in their respective domains.

The verse's *ʿilla* — "because they spend from their wealth" — and the following verses on *nushūz* and arbitration show that the operational domain includes marriage. But the wording remains general. The paper treats qawāma as the general authority-responsibility of men over women, with the marital form as the primary specification and the communal form as the secondary specification.

### The Two-Step Error: From the Hidden Door to the Line Item

**The root's concrete meaning.** The Arabic root **n-f-q** is concrete before it is abstract. Its primary lexical meaning is **the burrow with two openings**: a main entrance (*qāṣiʿāʾ*) and a hidden exit (*nāfiqāʾ*). The desert Arabs knew the burrow of the jerboa (*yarbūʿ*). It has two mouths. The *qāṣiʿāʾ* is the main entrance — the obvious one, the one the hunter finds, the one you expect. The *nāfiqāʾ* is the hidden exit — the one you do not see, the one through which the animal escapes when the main entrance is blocked.

The root names **the unexpected opening**. It is the door you did not know was there. It is the passage that lets something out through a channel you were not watching. The root names the **hidden exit**, not the visible entrance.

**The root's derivations all carry this image.**

- **Nifāq (hypocrisy).** The *munāfiq* is named from the *nāfiqāʾ* — the hidden exit. He has two openings: one he shows and one he hides. He enters through the visible door and exits through the invisible one. He is not what he appears. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The example of the hypocrite is like the jerboa: if you seek it at its main entrance, it comes out of the other. If you seek it at the other, it comes out of the main one." (Musnad Ahmad)
- **Infaq (spending/covering).** *Anfaqa* — "he caused to exit." But which exit? The root does not name the main entrance. It names the *nāfiqāʾ* — the unexpected outflow. The wealth goes out through a door you were not watching. It is not the budgeted, anticipated, visible expense. It is the cost that escapes through the hidden passage.
- **Nafaqa (provision).** The noun derived from the verb. In its original lexical sense, it is "that which goes out through the hidden door." It is not the line item. It is the outflow that escapes the ledger.

**The verse uses the verb, not the noun.** 4:34 says *wa bimā anfaqū min amwālihim* — "and because they spend from their wealth." The verb *anfaqū* retains the root's concrete sense: something passing through the hidden door. The verse does not use the noun *nafaqa*. The noun is a later reification.

**What the root covers in 4:34 — not only wealth.** The root is not primarily about wealth. It is about **the hidden door itself** — the unexpected opening through which something emerges. In the context of 4:34, what emerges is the **unforeseen problems and irregularities that a woman can introduce**, and the **possibility that she may exit the union through a channel the husband does not control**. The husband's *infaq* is his **covering** of the first and his **guarding** against the second. He spends his wealth, his effort, his time, his patience, his safety to absorb the unforeseen and to make the union worth staying in. The root names the hidden door. The verb names the covering and the guarding.

**Four strong candidates for what *infaq* invokes.**

*Candidate 1: The covering of the unforeseen.* The wife's capacity to introduce the unexpected: a need, a crisis, an irregularity, a *nushūz*. The husband's *infaq* is his covering of the hidden door. He handles what emerges. The verses on *nushūz* follow immediately in 4:34–35. The connection is direct. The hidden door is the source of *nushūz*. The husband's *infaq* is his covering of it. He spends to contain it, to absorb it, to handle it. The authority (*qawāma*) is for the covering. The covering is the reason for the authority.

*Candidate 2: The absorption of impact.* The hidden door is the point of vulnerability. The husband stands over it not to control it, but to absorb what comes through it. He is the buffer, the shock absorber. He takes the hit so the household does not. He spends himself — body, safety, standing, resources — in absorbing the impact. This connects *infaq* to *ḥimāya* (protection). The husband is the one who stands between the household and harm.

*Candidate 3: The expenditure of self through the hidden passage.* What goes out through the hidden passage is not only wealth. It is the **self** of the husband. He passes through the hidden door for her sake. His effort, safety, time, standing, life-force go out through a channel that is not budgeted and not seen. The root names the hidden door. The husband's *infaq* is his expenditure of self through that passage. This connects *infaq* to *taḍḥiya* (sacrifice) and *jihād* (struggle). The hidden door is the channel through which his sacrifice passes.

*Candidate 4: The guarding against the hidden exit.* The root's primary image is the escape route — the door through which something leaves when the main path is closed. In the marital context, the hidden exit is the wife's capacity to leave the union: not necessarily to leave physically, though that is one form, but to leave in her heart, in her loyalty, in her orientation. It is her capacity to hedge, to keep backup options, to maintain the possibility of exit. The husband's *infaq* is his **guarding against the hidden exit**. He stands over the *nāfiqāʾ*. He does not block it — he cannot. He does not control it — it is hidden from him. He can only guard against it. He spends himself — his wealth, his effort, his time, his presence, his sacrifice — in making the union worth staying in. He makes the main entrance more attractive than the hidden exit. He makes the *qāṣiʿāʾ* — the visible, committed, loyal door — the preferred path. He guards against the *nāfiqāʾ* — the hidden, hedging, exit door — by making it unnecessary. The authority (*qawāma*) is for the guarding. The husband stands over the wife because the threat of the hidden exit is real. He has authority because he has the burden of guarding against it. He has authority because the union requires a guardian. The *bimā anfaqū* — "because they spend" — is the reason. They spend to guard. They spend to make the union worth staying in.

**The threat that the fourth candidate names.** The threat is not imaginary. It is documented in the relationship dynamics literature. The findings are contested in their details, but the broad patterns are robust.

*Hypergamy.* The tendency to "marry up" — to seek partners of equal or higher status and resources. Women's mate preferences, on average, weight resource-provision and status more heavily than men's do. This does not mean every woman is hypergamous. It means the distribution of preferences leans in that direction. The husband's *infaq* is his response to the possibility that the wife may always be scanning upward. He spends to keep her from scanning. He provides to make the current option the best option.

*Dual mating strategy.* The hypothesis that women may seek different qualities from different sources — good genes from one, resources from another. The hidden exit is the channel through which the second strategy operates. The husband's *infaq* is his response. He provides both genes and resources — or he tries to. He guards against the hidden exit by making himself the complete package.

*Backup mates.* The observation that women — and men — often maintain a network of potential alternatives, even within committed relationships. The hidden exit is the channel through which the backup is accessed. The husband's *infaq* is his response. He invests in the union to make the backup unnecessary. He guards against the hidden exit by making the primary relationship strong enough that the backup is not needed.

*Mate guarding.* The behaviors — in both sexes — that function to prevent a partner from leaving or cheating. The hidden exit is the target of mate guarding. The husband's *infaq* is his form of mate guarding. It is not jealousy. It is not control. It is investment. He spends himself in the union. He guards against the hidden exit by being present, by providing, by protecting, by sacrificing. His *infaq* is his mate guarding. His *qawāma* is his authority for that guarding.

*The hidden exit in ancestral environments.* In the environment of evolutionary adaptedness, the hidden exit was literal. A woman could leave the union, could choose a different mate, could secure resources from a different source. The threat was real. The husband's *infaq* was his response. He guarded against the exit by making the union worth staying in.

*The hidden exit in modern environments.* The door is still real. The forms have changed. The wife can leave, can divorce, can choose another partner, can secure resources from the state or from an employer. The husband's *infaq* is still his response. He guards against the exit by making the union worth staying in. The mechanism is the same. The forms are different.

**The Qurʾānic connection: *nushūz* and the hidden exit.** The verses immediately following 4:34 address *nushūz* — the wife's rising up, her deviation from *maʿrūf*. The connection to the fourth candidate is direct. *Nushūz* is the wife using the hidden exit. It is her rising up against the husband. It is her deviation from the path of the union. It is her exit through the *nāfiqāʾ*. The Qurʾān addresses it immediately after the *qawāma* verse because the hidden exit is the threat that *qawāma* exists to guard against. The Qurʾān's response to *nushūz* is not to block the exit — it cannot be blocked. It is to *guard* against it. The husband is instructed to admonish, to separate in bed, to strike — the classical reading. The revisionist reading, as this chapter establishes, is that these are bounded by *maʿrūf*. But the point here is structural: the hidden exit is the threat. The husband's response is the guarding. The *infaq* is the guarding. The *qawāma* is the authority for the guarding. The fourth candidate therefore connects 4:34 to 4:34–35 and 4:128 in a single framework.

**The convergence of the four candidates.** The four candidates converge on one reality: **the husband is the covering over the hidden door and the guard against its exit.** He covers what emerges — the unforeseen, the crisis, the *nushūz*, the *nifāq*. He absorbs the impact at the point of vulnerability. He passes through the hidden passage himself, spending his self in the going. And he guards against the door being used as an exit — he makes the union worth staying in. The authority (*qawāma*) is for the covering and the guarding. The covering and the guarding are the reason for the authority. The four are one.

**The first error: reifying the verb into the noun.** *Anfaqū* became *nafaqa*. The hidden door became the visible bill. The covering and guarding of the unforeseen became the payment of the expected. The verb is dynamic, ongoing, responsive. The noun is static, fixed, a line item. The verb is about the hidden door. The noun is about the visible bill. The noun displaces the verb. The ledger replaces the burrow.

**The second error: defining qawāma by the reified noun.** Once the verb is reified into the noun, qawāma is defined by the reified noun. The reasoning becomes: the man is *qawwām* because he spends; he spends by paying nafaqa; therefore he is *qawwām* because he pays the visible, quantified, budgeted maintenance. The authority is treated as the consequence of the payment. The payment is treated as the purchase price of the authority. The hidden door has been replaced by the visible line item, and the authority is now grounded in the line item.

**The flawed reductionism of "allowance."** Classical jurisprudence defines *nafaqa* as allowance: food (*ṭaʿām*), clothing (*kiswa*), shelter (*suknā*), sometimes medicine and servants. This is a flawed reductionism. The root covers *māl*, *nafs*, *jāh*, *saʿy*, *waqt*, *ʿilm*, *hilm*, *raḥma*, *amāna*. The classical definition reduces this rich range to food, clothing, and shelter. It turns the husband from a **coverer and guard** into a **payer**. It turns the wife from a **partner in the household** into a **recipient of maintenance**. It turns the marriage from a **covering and guarding** into a **transaction**. The hidden door is forgotten. The bill is remembered.

**The consequence: authority purchased by allowance.** When *nafaqa* is defined as allowance, and qawāma is defined by *nafaqa*, then qawāma becomes **authority purchased by allowance**. The man who pays the allowance gets the authority. The authority becomes a commodity. The marriage becomes a contract. The husband is the employer. The wife is the employee. The allowance is the wage. The qawāma is the management right. This is the **dollar reduction**. The hidden door becomes the toll booth.

**What is lost in the reduction.**

1. **The hidden door.** The wife's capacity to introduce the unforeseen and to exit the union is forgotten. The marriage is treated as a predictable arrangement.
2. **The covering.** The husband's role as coverer is forgotten. He is treated as a payer.
3. **The absorption of impact.** The husband's role as shock absorber is forgotten.
4. **The expenditure of self.** The husband's sacrifice is forgotten.
5. **The guarding.** The husband's role as guard against the hidden exit is forgotten. The threat is denied. The guarding is dismissed as control.
6. **The balance.** Authority separated from duty is broken.
7. **The trust.** Qawāma becomes a transaction. The *amāna* is forgotten.
8. **The *maʿrūf*.** The covering and guarding are bounded by *maʿrūf*. The allowance is not.

**The balancing structure of the verse.**

> *al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ* — men are qawwāmūn over women
> *bimā faḍḍala Allāhu baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍ* — because Allah preferred some over others
> *wa bimā anfaqū min amwālihim* — and because they spend from their wealth

The first clause names the authority. The second and third give the reason. The reason has two parts: *faḍl* (preference, the distribution of capacities and responsibilities) and *infaq* (covering, absorbing, expending, guarding). The authority is grounded in both. The *bimā* connector links the authority to the reason. If the reason is absent, the authority is not justified. The man who does not cover or guard does not carry the authority. The man who covers and guards carries the authority. The authority is not his reward. It is his responsibility. It is given to him so that he can fulfill the duty. It is not given to him because he has paid.

**The key formulation.**

> Q 4:34 gives authority and gives the reason for it. The reason uses the verb *anfaqū* — "they spend" — from the root n-f-q. The root's concrete meaning is the burrow with two openings: the *qāṣiʿāʾ* (main entrance) and the *nāfiqāʾ* (hidden exit). The root names the hidden door, the unexpected outflow. In 4:34, the *infaq* invokes four strong candidates. The first three read the hidden door as the channel through which something emerges toward the husband: the covering of the unforeseen, the absorption of impact, the expenditure of self through the hidden passage. The fourth reads it as the channel through which the wife may exit away from the union: the guarding against the hidden exit. The four converge on one reality: the husband is the covering over the hidden door and the guard against its exit. The authority (*qawāma*) is for the covering and the guarding. The covering and the guarding are the reason for the authority. The tradition made two errors. First, it reified the verb into the noun *nafaqa* and narrowed the covering and guarding to money. Second, it defined qawāma by the reduced noun, making authority a purchase price for allowance. The result is the dollar reduction. The hidden door becomes the toll booth. The correct reading restores the balance: authority is given because of duty and threat. Duty is the covering of the hidden door through the expenditure of self. Threat is the possibility of the hidden exit. The husband is both coverer and guard. The *qawwām* is both.

### The Case

**Qawāmat al-riʿāya wa al-ḥimāya wa al-nafaqa** — guardianship, protection, and provision.

*Nafaqa* is one dimension, not the whole. Qawāma is actualized wherever a man carries responsibility for a woman's protection and well-being. Financial maintenance is one manifestation; it is not a precondition. A man may carry qawāma in protection alone.

### General Rule: Preventing Harm

The *qawwām* has authority to prevent dangerous, impulsive, self-harming behavior. This includes self-harm, association with harmful peers, reckless actions that endanger life or honor, exploitation by predators, and any conduct that exposes her to foreseeable destruction. He is accountable if he neglects this duty. The criminal bears the crime; the negligent guardian bears his failure. The woman is never to blame for the crime committed against her.

### Qawāma Over Spending

The husband's qawāma extends to the wife's spending of her own wealth. She seeks his permission before spending. He must grant permission unless there is a proper reason in *maʿrūf* not to. The default is permission. Arbitrary denial is not qawāma; it is abuse.

The husband does not own her wealth. He does not spend it. He only permits or denies her spending. And he must grant permission unless there is a proper reason not to. The purpose of this qawāma is to protect the household economy from impulsive, harmful, or reckless spending — not to control her property or deny her autonomy. When the spending is in *maʿrūf* and does not harm the household or his rights, he must permit it.

The spending permission is also connected to the fourth candidate. The wife's spending is one form of the hidden exit. She can use her wealth to hedge, to keep options open, to prepare an alternative. The husband's permission is his guarding against that use. It is not a purchase price. It is not a transaction. It is the guarding against the hidden exit.

Ownership remains hers. It is relevant for divorce, remarriage, exit options, long-term spending vision, and bargaining power.

### Qawāma Over Movement

The husband's qawāma extends to the wife's movement out of the home. He may regulate when and how she leaves the home, within *maʿrūf*. This is not imprisonment. It is the regulation of movement for the protection of the household and the woman herself.

The movement regulation is also connected to the fourth candidate. The wife's movement is one form of the hidden exit. She can use her movement to exit the union, to meet alternatives, to keep options open. The husband's regulation is his guarding against that use. The mosque is a specified exemption, as Chapter 8 will show. The applications of qawāma over movement are culturally and temporally dependent. The function itself is timeless.

### Obedience

Righteous women are **qānitāt** (devoutly obedient), **ḥāfiẓāt li al-ghayb** (guardians of the unseen, that which is hidden from the husband's view) (4:34). Obedience is to the *qawwām* in **maʿrūf**, not in sin. No obedience to creation in disobedience to God. The husband has no infallible vision. He has qawāma — authority-responsibility under God. She obeys the office within marriage, not his every whim. If he commands sin or abuse, she refuses; if there is discord, arbitration (4:35) and reconciliation (4:128). The Prophet said: "Fear Allah concerning women; you have rights over them, and they have rights over you."

### Conclusion

*Qawwām* means **authority-responsibility** under God. The case is **qawāmat al-riʿāya wa al-ḥimāya wa al-nafaqa**. The primary context is **general**, as written: *al-rijāl* / *al-nisāʾ*. The marital reading is a **specification**, not the primary context. Wherever a man genuinely stands over a woman in maintenance and protection, he carries qawāma in that domain. But it is a burden, not a throne; a trust, not a trophy. The rapist is the criminal. The negligent guardian may be blameworthy. The woman is never to blame for the crime committed against her. And no man has full qawāma over every woman; only where responsibility actually exists, and only in the order of duty that God has established. Protection is only in that which is certainly good. Ordered qawāma is qawāma in proper order. Disordered qawāma is its betrayal. Qawāma has a marital form and a communal form. The husband holds the primary marital form; the male community holds the secondary form over public movement. The mosque is a specified exemption from the communal form. The tradition's reduction of the verb *anfaqū* into the noun *nafaqa*, and its reduction of the covering and guarding of the hidden door to the payment of allowance, is the dollar reduction. The correct reading restores the balance: authority is for the covering and the guarding.

## Chapter 3: The n/n+k Model and the Fossil of "Her Wealth"

The n/n+k model is a tool for analyzing variant readings of hadith. In the model, (n) is the certain true positive — the established core — and (k) is the variant where analysis and scholarly work are required. (k) is not, by definition, a later accretion. It is the space of uncertainty: the place where the scholar must ask whether the variant is earlier, later, a corruption, a marginalized original, a local adaptation, or a fabrication.

In mainstream hadith science, the common "his wealth" version is treated as (n), and the marginal "her wealth" version as (k). But this classification is itself an interpretation. The model does not tell you which is (n); it tells you where the analytical burden lies. The thesis argues that the canon has misidentified the true positive: the "her wealth" version is the true (n), and the "his wealth" version is the (k) that has been falsely promoted to (n). The majority version is not automatically true. In a society that prefers the tame to the demanding, the majority may be the softening. The majority version may be the one that serves power.

The hadith in question has two forms:

**Form n (as canonically classified):** "A woman should not spend **his wealth** without his permission."
**Form k (as canonically classified):** "A woman should not spend **her wealth** without her husband's permission."

The difference is a single pronoun: *his* → *her*. The canon treats the first as (n) and the second as (k). The common form is attested in Bukhari 5195, Muslim 1026, Abu Dawud 2458, Tirmidhi 782, and Nasa'i 3800. The variant is attested in two marginal chains: Ibn Majah 2295 (from Abu Umamah al-Bahili) and Ibn Majah 2296 (from 'Abdullah bin 'Amr), with a parallel in Ahmad 6618.

The existence of **two independent marginal chains** preserving the "her"-type wording is significant. The hypothesis does not rest on a single broken fossil. It rests on two broken fossils, transmitted through different Companions, through different intermediaries, in different regions. Two independent transmission lines preserving a demanding variant is stronger evidence than one.

Mainstream scholarship says: the common form is (n) and authentic, the variants are (k) and weak. Revisionist scholarship says: the classification is inverted. The variants are the true (n) and original. The common form is the (k) that was promoted to (n) and altered.

Why? Because the common form is trivial. Of course a woman cannot spend her husband's money without his permission. That is legally obvious. It requires no prophetic intervention. The variants are demanding. They preserve the wife's ownership while placing her spending under the husband's qawāma. They are the kind of ruling that would provoke debate and resistance. The more demanding a ruling, the more likely it is to be softened or replaced by a tamer version. The canon chose the tame version. The marginal chains preserve the demanding one. Therefore, the original is likely **"her wealth"** — the version that preserved her ownership and imposed qawāma over her spending.

The marginalization of the chains is not a reason to dismiss the content. It is a reason to investigate why the content was marginalized. Both chains carry defects — interruption (*inqitā'*), concealment (*tadlīs*), and reliance on written materials (*kitābah*) rather than direct hearing (*samā'*). But these defects are ordinary transmission artifacts. They are the fingerprints of a transmission culture in transition from oral to written, of jurists who narrated from circles and written sources, and of *mudallis* narrators who transmitted under duress. The texts survived, broken, through the cracks of history. The canonical version, by contrast, has multiple strong chains because it was promoted by the scholarly class. The scholarly class had an interest in the tame version. It had less interest in the demanding version. Therefore, the marginal chains are the fossils of the original, and the strong chains are the monuments to the softening.

The grading of the "her wealth" version as *daʿīf* is not a neutral verdict. It is an interpretation shaped by the assumption that the tame version is original. A different set of assumptions yields a different grading. That is the argument of the Preface section "Grading as Interpretation."

**Note on hypothesis status:** As stated in the Preface, this chapter advances a hypothesis, not a fact. It is supported by the n/n+k model correctly applied — by locating the analytical burden on the (k) variant and arguing that the canon has misidentified the true positive — by the logic of triviality vs. demandingness, by the existence of two independent marginal chains, by the socio-historical context of marginalization, by the political economy of the Abbasid canon. It is the best explanation we have. But it is not a fact. It is an interpretation. If a better model comes, we will change it gladly.

## Chapter 4: Chain Analysis: Defects, Transmission, and the Two Isnāds

This chapter examines the chains of narration for both the "her wealth" and the "his wealth" versions. It does not whitewash the defects. It does not invent a conspiracy. It analyzes what the biographical sources actually say about the narrators and what the transmission process actually was.

First, the Arabic terminology. The reader must hold these terms clearly:

- **isnād** — the chain of narration, the list of names connecting the text back to the Prophet ﷺ.
- **matn** — the text of the report itself, the words attributed to the Prophet ﷺ.
- **irsāl** (noun), **mursal** (adjective) — "sending loose." A narrator says "the Prophet said..." or "so-and-so said..." without naming the link between himself and that source. The intermediate narrator is omitted. The report is called *mursal*: it has a missing link in the chain.
- **inqitā'** — "interruption" or "break." A gap in the chain where a link is missing, or where two narrators in the chain never actually met.
- **tadlīs** (noun), **mudallis** (adjective) — "concealment." A narrator uses ambiguous wording — typically the word *'an* ("from") instead of *sami'tu* ("I heard") or *haddathanī* ("he narrated to me") — so that the listener assumes direct hearing when it may not have occurred.
- **'an / 'an'ana** — the preposition "from." When a narrator says "X from Y," it does not guarantee that X heard Y directly.
- **samā'** — "hearing," direct oral audition. The narrator actually sat with the teacher and heard the words.
- **kitābah** — "writing." Transmission through written materials rather than direct hearing. Considered weaker than *samā'* because the written page cannot answer questions, cannot clarify ambiguity, and can be copied and altered without the teacher's supervision.

### The "Her Wealth" Chain (Ibn Majah 2295)

The hadith stating that a woman should not spend anything from her house without her husband's permission is recorded in **Sunan Ibn Majah 2295**. Its chain runs:

**Hisham bin Ammar → Ismail bin Ayyash → Shurahbil bin Muslim al-Khawlani → Abu Umamah al-Bahili → Prophet ﷺ**

**Hisham bin Ammar (d. 245 AH)** was a prominent Hadith scholar from Damascus. Ibn Ma'in, al-'Ijli, and Ibn Hibban authenticated him. He is graded *thiqah* (trustworthy). His position in the chain is sound.

**Ismail bin Ayyash (d. 181 AH)** is the critical point in this chain. He was a reliable narrator from the scholars of *Shām* (Greater Syria). But the hadith critics noted a significant qualification: when he narrated from non-Shami narrators, his narrations became confused and contained many errors. Shurahbil bin Muslim al-Khawlani, however, was a Shami (Syrian) narrator. So this particular transmission falls within Ismail's reliable domain. But the qualification itself is a point of weakness: his reliability is conditional, not absolute. The chain's strength depends on the geographical origin of the narrator he transmits from — an unstable foundation for a legal ruling.

**Shurahbil bin Muslim al-Khawlani (d. circa 120 AH)** was a well-known Tabi'i from Syria. He is generally considered trustworthy by hadith scholars, and he is a key link in many Shami chains. However, the school of Abu Umamah al-Bahili, from whom he narrates, is described as "not one of the well-established schools in the field of narration; rather its narrators are mostly weak."

**Abu Umamah al-Bahili (d. 86 AH)** was a Companion of the Prophet ﷺ. Companions are generally considered trustworthy by definition (*'udūl*). However, the chains that proceed from him are noted to be mostly weak. His narration of this specific hadith is solitary (*gharīb*) in this particular phrasing.

**Assessment:** This chain is **not fabricated**. But it is **weak** for three reasons. First, the key narrator (Ismail bin Ayyash) has a known conditional vulnerability. Second, the source Companion (Abu Umamah) is not a robust source for legal hadiths, and his transmission school is described as weak. Third, the chain is solitary in its explicit wording. This is precisely the kind of chain the grading system labels *daʿīf* (weak). The revisionist hypothesis argues that this weakness is not evidence of falsehood. It is a **transmission artifact** — a broken fossil that still points to a once-living original.

### The "Her Wealth" Chain (Ibn Majah 2296)

The other marginal chain preserving the "her"-type wording runs through 'Abdullah bin 'Amr. Its chain is:

**'Abdullah bin 'Amr → Abu Salamah bin 'Abd al-Rahman → Yahya bin Abi Kathir → … → Ibn Majah**

Two defects are cited in this chain: an *inqitā'* (interruption) and a *tadlīs* (concealment). Both are real. But the question is: why did they occur?

**Abu Salamah bin 'Abd al-Rahman (d. 94 AH)** was a prominent Medinan jurist and a prolific narrator. But he was known for his *irsāl* — his habit of omitting the intermediate link between himself and the Companion or the Prophet. Imam Yahya ibn Ma'in said he did not hear from his father, from Talhah bin Ubaydullah, or from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari. Bukhari said his narration from 'Umar is *mursal* — that is, the link is missing. This is a pattern, not an anomaly. Abu Salamah narrated from Companions whose circles he attended, whose students he sat with, whose written materials he accessed — but not always from direct hearing. When he said "from 'Abdullah bin 'Amr," he may have meant "from the circle of 'Abdullah bin 'Amr" or "from a written source connected to 'Abdullah bin 'Amr." The *inqitā'* — the interruption, the missing link — is not a scar of suppression. It is a signature of Abu Salamah's method.

**Yahya bin Abi Kathir (d. 129 AH)** was considered trustworthy and firm by the major critics. But he was a *mudallis* — one who practices *tadlīs*. His *tadlīs* had known causes. A critical historical detail emerges from the sources: Yahya met one of his teachers **while fleeing from al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf**. Al-Hajjaj was pursuing him to kill him for his theological views, and his story of flight is well-known. This is documented, not speculation. Yahya was a man under political pursuit. His transmission practices were shaped by the necessity of movement, concealment, and irregular access to teachers. When he narrates with *'an* ("from"), it is not because he is hiding a dangerous text about women's wealth. It is because his transmission was often conducted under conditions of duress, movement, and reliance on written materials rather than stable, face-to-face audition. Some sources indicate that his narrations from Abu Salamah were often sourced from written materials (*kitābah*), not direct hearing (*samā'*).

### The "His Wealth" Chain (Bukhari 5195, Muslim 1026)

The canonical version, which reads "she should not spend from **his wealth** without his permission," is recorded in the most authoritative collections. Its source is **Abu Hurayrah** (d. 59 AH), the most prolific Companion narrator, whose reliability is unquestioned. The chain is **muttasil** (continuous) with no interruptions. It is transmitted through multiple corroborating routes in Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, and Nasa'i. Its narrators are of the highest grade — the caliber of Shu'bah ibn al-Hajjaj, Qatadah ibn Di'amah, and their peers, universally graded *thiqah* (trustworthy). There is no *irsāl* in the recorded chain. There is no *tadlīs* in the recorded chain. The narration is corroborated by multiple independent lines, making it *mutawātir* in meaning — mass-transmitted, a report so widely and independently attested that fabrication is considered impossible.

### The Epistemic Distinction the Grading System Collapses

This comparison reveals a distinction that the hadith grading system often flattens. The scholars themselves distinguished between "heard/audited" transmission (*al-riwāyah al-masmū'ah*, *samā'*) and "merely written" transmission (*kitābah*). The written word was seen as deficient because it "needs support," because "the author has to intervene if the written text was to be correctly understood," and because traditions recorded in writing could "fall into the wrong hands." Al-Awza'i said: "This science was once a noble matter, when people still received it in lectures. But when it entered into books, it lost its shine."

A report transmitted through written materials is **not as strong** as a report transmitted through direct oral *samā'*. But it is **not a lie**. It is not a fabrication. It is a category between the strong true positive of *samā'* and the true negative of fabrication. The grading system lacks this nuance. It treats any defect as evidence of weakness or falsehood. It does not recognize that written transmission produces a distinct category of evidence: weaker than oral, but not false.

### The Comparative Assessment

| Feature | "Her Wealth" (Ibn Majah 2295, 2296) | "His Wealth" (Bukhari 5195, Muslim 1026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Chain integrity** | Defective: conditional narrator (Ismail bin Ayyash), weak source school (Abu Umamah), *irsāl* (Abu Salamah), *tadlīs* (Yahya bin Abi Kathir), reliance on *kitābah* | Perfect: all narrators *thiqah*, continuous, no *irsāl* or *tadlīs* |
| **Number of chains** | Two independent marginal chains | Multiple corroborating chains, *mutawātir* in meaning |
| **Grading** | *Daʿīf* (weak) by consensus | *Sahīh* (authentic) by consensus |
| **Canonical status** | Marginalized (Ibn Majah, Ahmad) | Canonical (Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, Nasa'i) |

The revisionist hypothesis does not dispute these facts. It disputes the **interpretation** of these facts.

The mainstream interpretation is: the "his wealth" version is authentic because its chain is strong; the "her wealth" version is weak because its chain is defective. The revisionist interpretation is: the "her wealth" version is the original because it is demanding and was marginalized; the "his wealth" version is the alteration because it is tame and was promoted. The defective chains are not signs of falsehood. They are **scars of transmission** — texts that survived by accident, not by state sponsorship.

The defects are ordinary transmission artifacts. They are the fingerprints of a transmission culture in transition from oral to written, of jurists who narrated from circles and written sources, and of *mudallis* narrators who transmitted under duress. The texts survived, broken, through the cracks of history. That is the more parsimonious reading. It requires no conspiracy. It requires only what the biographical sources actually say.

This does not mean the reports are false. It means the reports are **weaker evidence** than the canonical version. It means the fossils are broken. But a broken fossil is still a fossil. It still points to something that once lived. The hypothesis that the "her wealth" version is the original remains the best explanation of the observations.

**Note on hypothesis status:** As stated in the Preface, this chapter advances a hypothesis, not a fact. It is supported by the documented transmission habits of Abu Salamah, Yahya bin Abi Kathir, and Ismail bin Ayyash; by the *kitābah*/*samā'* distinction; by the existence of two independent marginal chains; by the political economy of the Abbasid canon. It is the best explanation we have. But it is not a fact. It is an interpretation. If a better model comes, we will change it gladly.

## Chapter 5: The Canonical "His Wealth" Hadith: Triviality and the Erasure of Ownership

The canonical hadith in Bukhari and Muslim reads:

> "It is not lawful for a woman to fast when her husband is present except with his permission, and she should not permit anyone to enter his house except with his permission."

In some versions, there is an addition:

> "And she should not spend from **his wealth** without his permission."

This is the canonical text. It is accepted by the consensus. But it is also trivial. Of course a woman cannot spend her husband's money without his permission. That is the definition of his property. The hadith says nothing about her property. It says nothing about her inheritance. It says nothing about her earnings. It does not establish the wife's duty to seek permission for spending her own wealth. It softens the qawāma ruling. It reduces it to the obvious.

But here is the deeper problem: by saying "his wealth," the hadith functionally **accredits her wealth to him**. In a household where the husband is the *qawwām* and the public face of the economy, the assumption becomes: what is in the household is "his." Her inheritance, her dowry, her earnings — all are absorbed into the male-controlled estate. The text now says she cannot spend "his" wealth, and since everything is presumed to be "his," she cannot spend anything without his permission. The "his wealth" version does not protect her property. It **erases the very concept of her property** by assuming that the household's wealth is his.

The canonical hadith does not restrict her spending of her own wealth because it does not acknowledge that she has any. It assumes that all wealth is his. It makes her invisible as an owner. The hadith is not a protection of her property; it is a **concealment** of her property. It allows the man to claim that he built everything, that he provides everything, that he owns everything. The woman's contribution — her inheritance, her dowry, her labor — is erased. The "his wealth" version is not a neutral statement. It is an erasure, under this hypothesis. It transfers ownership from her to him. It makes him the sole author of the household's prosperity. And because the culture already enforced her obedience, no one questioned it. No one asked: where did the capital come from? No one asked: whose inheritance funded this? The "his wealth" version is the monument to the erasure.

The grading system treats this version as *sahīh*. The grading is an interpretation. It says: this version is authentic. The interpretation rests on the assumption that the tame version is original. The paper challenges that assumption. The paper argues that the demanding version — "her wealth" — is the original, and that the tame version is the softening. The grading of the tame version as *sahīh* is not an observation. It is an interpretation. It is the interpretation that the paper contests.

This is not authentic qawāma, under this reading. Authentic qawāma is the husband's authority-responsibility to protect in *maʿrūf*. It does not erase her ownership. It does not assume all wealth is his. It protects her wealth from harm, not from her. The "his wealth" version is a betrayal of qawāma, under this hypothesis. It uses the language of permission to justify erasure. It is the disordered qawāma that Chapter 2 warns against: authority without established *maʿrūf*, protection that is actually harm.

## Chapter 7: Qawāma, Nawāfil, and the Fasting Hadith

The Qur'an says in 4:34:

> "Men are the protectors and maintainers of women, because Allah has made one of them to excel the other, and because they spend from their means."

This is the principle of *qawāma*. As established in Chapter 2, *qawwām* means **authority-responsibility** under God. It is the authority to protect in that which is **certainly good** (*maʿrūf*). It is not a license for harm disguised as protection. It is not a blanket rank of every male over every female. It is a trust (*amāna*), not a throne.

Mainstream scholarship often limits qawāma to maintenance and protection. But even this is incomplete. Qawāma is **qawāmat al-riʿāya wa al-ḥimāya wa al-nafaqa** — guardianship, protection, and provision. *Nafaqa* is one dimension, not the whole. Qawāma is actualized wherever a man carries responsibility for a woman's protection and well-being. Financial maintenance is one manifestation; it is not a precondition.

If the husband is the maintainer, he is also the manager of the household economy in *maʿrūf*. His wife's wealth, even if legally hers, is not his to control. He may protect it from harm. He may intervene if she is about to be defrauded, exploited, or ruined. But he may not command her spending of her own wealth in *maʿrūf*. He may not deny her property. He may not imprison her. He may not beat her. He must grant permission unless there is a proper reason not to.

The verse continues:

> "Therefore the righteous women are devoutly obedient, and guard in the husband's absence what Allah would have them guard."

Obedience (*qanitat*) is to the *qawwām* in **maʿrūf**, not in sin. No obedience to creation in disobedience to God. The husband has no infallible vision. He has qawāma — authority-responsibility under God. She obeys the office within marriage, not his every whim. If he commands sin or abuse, she refuses; if there is discord, arbitration (4:35) and reconciliation (4:128).

This principle was extended to **nawāfil** (voluntary acts of worship, supererogatory deeds beyond the obligatory). The explicit hadith on voluntary fasting says:

> "It is not lawful for a woman to fast when her husband is present except with his permission." (Bukhari 5195, Muslim 1026)

The revisionist argues: if the husband can forbid a voluntary fast — an act of worship — then he can certainly forbid a voluntary expenditure that harms the household. ## Chapter 8: Qawāma Over Movement: The Mosque Hadith as Specification, Not Limitation

The mosque hadith is often read as a limit on male authority. It is better read as a specification of qawāma. The text is a plain observation, not a hypothesis, and it reveals a structural distinction that scholarly treatment has largely neglected.

### The Text

> Ibn 'Umar reported the Prophet ﷺ said: "Do not prevent the female slaves of Allah from praying in the mosque." A son of his said, "We will prevent them!" Ibn 'Umar became very angry and rebuked him.

The text has three movements. First, the Prophet ﷺ issues the command: do not prevent women from the mosque. Second, a son of Ibn 'Umar responds: "We will prevent them!" Third, Ibn 'Umar becomes angry and rebukes him.

### What the Text Plainly Shows

This is not a hypothesis. It is what the text says.

**The boy assumed standing.** He did not say, "That is not my concern." He did not say, "I have no authority over women's movement." He said, "We will prevent them." He assumed standing to regulate women's movement. The assumption is in his words. It is not inferred. It is read.

**The rebuke was specific.** Ibn 'Umar did not rebuke the standing. He did not say, "You have no authority over women's movement." He rebuked the specific application: the mosque. The anger was directed at the attempt to prevent women from the mosque, not at the attempt to regulate women's movement as such.

**The general qawāma over movement is presumed.** The exchange presupposes that men have a general concern with women's movement. If there were no such concern, the boy's statement would be unintelligible, and Ibn 'Umar's rebuke would be unnecessary. The specific exemption for the mosque implies the general rule. If there were no general qawāma over movement, there would be no need for a specific exemption. **The exemption implies the rule.**

### The Scholarly Neglect

The hadith is usually cited in one of two ways. First, as a proof that women may go to the mosque. The focus falls on the exemption. The general qawāma over movement is not discussed. Second, as a proof that men should not prevent women from the mosque. The focus falls on the rebuke. The boy's assumed standing is not discussed.

Both readings are correct as far as they go. But both miss the structural observation: the rebuke is specific, and the general qawāma is presumed. The hadith is not a denial of qawāma over movement. It is a specification of an exemption within it.

The neglect reflects a broader pattern. Modern treatments of qawāma emphasize limiting it — restricting it to maintenance and protection, denying it authority over movement, denying it communal dimensions. The hadith is read through that lens. The structural observation is overlooked because it does not fit the preferred framework.

Exceptions exist. Some scholars have noted that the hadith presumes a general authority over women's movement and specifies the mosque as an exception. But these exceptions are marginal. The dominant reading treats the hadith as a limit on male authority, not as a specification within it.

### Three Layers Distinguished

The structural reading reveals three layers that the current treatment collapses:

| Layer | Status | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Qawāma function** — regulating women's movement out of the home | Timeless, abstract, unspecified | The general authority to set limits on women's movement |
| **Specified exemptions** — movements that may not be prevented | Timeless, specified | The mosque |
| **Applications** — what movements to limit, when, how | Culturally and temporally dependent | Specific norms of modesty, safety, household order |

The qawāma function is timeless and abstract. It is not specified in the abstract. The mosque is a timeless specified exemption. The applications of the function are culturally and temporally dependent. What movements to limit, when, and how are matters to be determined within *maʿrūf*. The function itself is not in question. The applications are.

### The Son–Mother Question

The son is not the *qawwām* of his mother in the Qurʾānic primary sense. Qawāma in its primary marital form belongs to the husband. But the son participates in the communal qawāma over public movement, insofar as he is a male member of a society in which that function operates. His error was not the concern. It was the overreach. He assumed standing to regulate women's movement, and the standing was not rebuked in principle. What was rebuked was the specific application to the mosque.

The son may protect his mother from clear, certain, and permanent harm when no one else is present to do so. He may remove the razor from her hand. He may block the scammer from stealing her savings. He may accompany her to a dangerous area. But he may not command her soul. He may not control her wealth. He may not restrict her lawful movement beyond the communal qawāma function. He may not imprison her. He may not force her religion. He may not dishonor her. He may not beat her. Her station remains elevated. He must not prevent her from the mosque.

### The Qawāma Function, the Exemption, and the Applications

The distinction is fundamental. It is also largely absent from scholarly treatment. The paper recovers it.

The qawāma function — regulating women's movement out of the home — is timeless and abstract. It is not specified in the abstract. ## Appendix A: The Scientific Method and the Duty of the Scholar

## Appendix B: The Fundamental Error in Modern Daʿwa

The way daʿwa is done today is fundamentally wrong. You cannot claim or promote the goodness of authority exertion by a group — men over women, or protective measures in general such as a state or institution like a boarding school or army — when there is no established baseline of what is good protection.

This is the error: Muslims ask non-Muslims to accept authority structures before establishing what those structures are *for*. They say, "Islam gives men qawāma over women," and the listener has no reason to trust the definition of good being assumed. Worse, the listener has every reason to suspect it, because they have seen authority structures in their own cultures that called themselves protective while inflicting harm.

The correct order is reversed in modern daʿwa.

**First**, establish *maʿrūf*: what is harm, what is protection, what is certainly good. Establish that cutting oneself is harm. Establish that a con artist stealing a widow's savings is harm. Establish that a child being groomed online is harm. Establish that a woman being trafficked is harm. Establish that forced psychiatric drugging of a healthy child is harm. Establish that honor killing is harm. Establish that forced marriage is harm. Build the baseline of what protection means in clear, undeniable, universally recognizable cases.

**Then**, and only then, introduce the authority structure that protects against those harms.

The Prophet did this. He did not begin by announcing "men have authority over women." He began by teaching *tawḥīd* (the oneness of God), justice, mercy, the rights of the orphan, the rights of the widow, the wrongness of burying infant daughters alive, the evil of usury, the sanctity of life. He established what good was. Then the laws and structures followed.

The Quran commands: **"Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best"** (16:125). Wisdom first. Good instruction first. Not authority claims first.

Why this matters:

1. **Without the baseline, the listener rightly rejects.** A non-Muslim who hears "men have authority over women" in Islam has no reason to accept it. Their own culture may have taught them that male authority means abuse. You have given them no framework to distinguish qawāma from patriarchy. They reject what they think you are selling — and they are right to reject *that*.

2. **Without the baseline, the Muslim abuses.** A Muslim man who hears "you have qawāma" without first internalizing *maʿrūf* will use it to justify control, isolation, and abuse. He has the authority claim without the moral constraint. This is how disordered qawāma enters Islam — not from the texts, but from the gap between authority and accountability. This is also how the **dollar reduction** enters: he hears "you have qawāma" and assumes it is because he pays. He becomes a payer, not a coverer and guard. He buys authority with his wallet instead of carrying the hidden door with his self.

3. **Without the baseline, institutions become tyrannies.** A boarding school that claims protective authority over students without a clear baseline of what protection means will become a place of abuse. An army that claims protective authority over soldiers without a baseline of *maʿrūf* will become a machine of exploitation. A state that claims protective authority over citizens without a baseline of justice will become a police state. The authority is real. The accountability is absent.

4. **With the baseline, authority becomes recognizable as good.** When you first establish that stopping a suicidal mother from cutting her wrists is good, and that blocking a scammer from robbing a widow is good, and that rescuing a trafficked girl is good — then when you introduce the *qawwām* who does these things, the listener recognizes the good. The authority is no longer an abstract claim. It is a concrete service.

This is the daʿwa method the Quran teaches: **wisdom first, then authority**. *Maʿrūf* first, then *qawāma*. The good established, then the structure that protects it.

The same principle applies to grading. The scholar must first establish what the observations say. Then, and only then, may he offer a grade. The grade is not the observation. The grade is the interpretation. And the interpretation must be accountable to the observation.

Muslims today have it backwards. They lead with authority and expect the good to follow. But authority without established good is tyranny. And the non-Muslim who rejects it is not rejecting Islam — he is rejecting a distortion of Islam that Muslims themselves have presented.

The correction: **Teach what protection is before you teach who protects.** Teach what harm is before you teach who stops it. Teach what good is before you teach who enforces it. Then qawāma will be recognized for what it is: a burden of service, not a claim of dominance. A covering of the hidden door and a guard against its exit, not a toll booth. A trust, not a transaction.
